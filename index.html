01

Executive Summary

UX design looks different than it did three or four years ago. Back then, a UX designer's or product manager's primary focus was manual execution: wireframing, prototyping, and producing deliverables by hand. Today, AI can take on much of that execution, and the required skill set is shifting toward high-level design evaluation and functional prototyping.

That shift doesn't remove the designer from the process. AI can speed up nearly every stage of UX work, but it still needs human oversight to catch errors, question its output, and keep the user at the center of every decision.

This guide shows where that balance lies. Organized around the , it walks through each stage of the UX process, from discovery and research to prototyping and handoff. At each stage, it identifies which tasks AI can handle, which ones call for human judgment, and how to work with AI as a junior partner rather than a replacement.

02

AI as the Junior Partner

Ever since AI took off, there's one question I've heard more times than I can count. It comes from fellow UX designers, from web developers, and, honestly, from anyone whose job involves a computer: "Where do I fit into all this?" Or, more bluntly: "Is AI going to replace me?"

It's a question I ask myself too, from time to time.

Reasonable as the question is, I think the answer is quite simple. The fact that AI is quick on its feet, more than us humans are or will ever be, is not up for debate. However, without training, AI has no context about your users, and even with that training, it still can't read a room, and is sometimes confidently wrong.

Accordingly, the real question isn't whether AI will replace designers. It's how designers should work with it. The model I keep coming back to is the junior designer: someone who is fast, eager, tireless, and capable of producing a surprising amount of work, but who doesn't yet know your users, your business, or when something "technically correct" is actually wrong.

You wouldn't hand a junior designer a project and ship whatever comes back. You'd brief them clearly, give them context, review their work, and push back when something misses the mark. Working with AI is no different. This approach has a name: , meaning a person stays involved at the decision points rather than letting AI run unchecked from start to finish.

Three ground rules for working with your junior partner

  1. Brief it like a colleague. AI output is only as good as the context you give it. Vague prompts produce generic results.
  2. Verify everything. AI can , presenting invented facts, quotes, or statistics with total confidence. Treat its output as a first draft, never a finished product.
  3. You own the outcome. If an AI-generated design excludes users or misrepresents research, that's on the team, not the tool. This also means never pasting into an AI tool your organization hasn't approved.

How to use this guide. The remaining chapters follow the four phases of the Double Diamond framework: Discover, Define, Develop, and Deliver. Each chapter shows which tasks to delegate, which to keep, a sample prompt you can adapt, and the risks to watch for.

03 Discover

Discovery & Research

Discovery is the process of going wide to understand users, their needs, pain points, the context in which they live and how all that shapes their experience with a system or product. During this phase, it can be very tempting to use AI because of how speedy its turnaround can be, but it's also arguably the most dangerous phase to use AI in, because everything that follows during the design process hinges on getting this stage right.

It's not entirely "wrong" to use AI during this stage, but it's recommended that the designer draws a clear picture of what parts of this process they should own and what parts AI can handle.

Who handles what during discovery
AI can handleThe designer should own
Drafting interview guides and survey questionsConducting interviews and reading body language, hesitation, and emotion
Summarizing interview transcriptsDeciding which insights actually matter
Converting notes from user interviews to themesSorting notes by the underlying meaning the users had
Conducting competitive scans and desk researchRecruiting interview participants

Sample prompt

You are a UX researcher helping me synthesize interview notes. Below are anonymized notes from 6 interviews with solo concert-goers who are top listeners of an artist on Spotify about how they decide whether to attend a concert with people they've met through an app. Group the observations into 4–6 themes. For each theme, list the supporting observations and which participant they came from (P1–P6). Do not add any observations that are not in the notes. If something is ambiguous, flag it rather than interpreting it.

The last two sentences are doing all the heavy lifting because contrary to the rest of the prompt which tells AI what tasks to do, the two sentences tell it what not to do, which narrows the space for AI hallucination thus making the output checkable.

Glossary

Affinity diagramming
A method for organizing research observations into groups based on shared themes.
Agentic AI
AI systems that can plan and carry out multi-step tasks with some independence, such as navigating software or editing files.
AI hallucination
When an AI produces false or invented information and presents it as fact.
AI slop
Low-quality, generic AI-generated content produced in volume without meaningful human review.
Context window
The amount of text an AI model can consider at one time; information beyond it may be lost or ignored.
Deskilling
The loss of skills that happens when people rely on automation for tasks they used to do themselves.
Double Diamond framework
A design process model with four phases (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver), alternating between broad exploration and narrow focus.
Generative AI (GenAI)
AI that creates new content, such as text, images, or code, based on patterns in its training data.
HITL (Human-in-the-Loop)
An approach where people stay involved in reviewing and approving AI decisions.
Information architecture (IA)
The structure and organization of content so users can find what they need.
Microcopy
Short interface text, such as button labels, error messages, and form hints.
PII (Personally Identifiable Information)
Any data that could identify a specific person, such as names, emails, or addresses.
Prompt engineering
The practice of writing and refining instructions to get useful output from AI.
WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines)
International standards for making web content accessible to people with disabilities.
